The cornea is the clear, sensitive front surface of the eye. If it is injured, inflamed or becomes
cloudy, this usually shows as a red, watering or squinted eye.

## When you should see us

- a suddenly painful, watering or squinted eye
- visible cloudiness or a whitish, greyish or dark spot on the cornea
- a corneal defect that does not heal despite treatment by your family vet
- chronic, sticky eye discharge or recurring conjunctivitis

## Common conditions

- injuries and ulcers of the cornea, including deep or already perforated defects
- persistent, non-healing defects such as SCCED ("boxer ulcer")
- chronic inflammation, for example the hereditary pannus disease in German Shepherds
- infections, among others with feline herpesvirus
- cornea nigra (corneal sequestrum) – a blackish tissue necrosis that occurs mainly in short-nosed cat
  breeds
- dermoids – congenital, hairy islands of skin on the cornea that rub constantly
- dry eye and foreign bodies

## What we examine

We stain the corneal surface with a special dye (fluorescein) – defects show up green, even when they
are barely visible to the naked eye.

## Treatment

Depending on the findings, treatment ranges from eye drops and a minor procedure under local anaesthesia
– for example roughening the wound edges of non-healing defects, sometimes with a fine diamond burr
(corneal debridement) – to surgery under general anaesthesia. For deep or already perforated defects we
cover the cornea with the animal's own tissue (conjunctival or corneoconjunctival graft) or stabilise it
temporarily with a special contact lens or an amniotic membrane product until the wound can bear the
load again on its own.

More about individual corneal conditions – such as the melting corneal ulcer or herpes infection in
cats – can be found in our [knowledge base](/wissen/) (in German).
